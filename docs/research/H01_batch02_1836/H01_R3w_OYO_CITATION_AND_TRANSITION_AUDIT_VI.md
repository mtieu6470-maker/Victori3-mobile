## 2. What Asiwaju p.704 actually establishes

The chapter's pp.703–704 says that around 1800 Old Oyo was the most important power across much of the Mono–Niger region. It distinguishes:
- an Oyo core that “hardly extended” beyond localities between the Osun and Ogun river basins;
- a wider area from which tribute came and where military, political, economic and cultural influence or control of varying degrees operated, approximating the western half of the chapter's much larger Mono–Niger study area;
- Dahomey as a tributary from 1748 until the early 1820s; Porto Novo as Oyo's port; Egba and Egbado areas under varying forms of Oyo officials, settlement and trade penetration;
- Ketu and Sabe preserving political autonomy while maintaining cordial/protected relations; eastern Borgu and south-western Nupe acknowledging the Alafin; and other eastern Yoruba areas subject to uneven influence and, at times, repulsed Oyo military pressure.

This is strong evidence that “Oyo influence/tribute space” is not interchangeable with a direct-rule census. It is not a local population total, and it does not validate a 750,000 number. It is also a c.1800/peak-era context, not a direct 1 January 1836 count.

## 3. Oyo collapse and population movement

Asiwaju's same chapter gives a sequence rather than a one-day event:
- Old Oyo was the leading power around 1800, but structural decline and military/territorial vulnerability were already evident in the eighteenth century.
- The chapter dates the empire's collapse to c.1835, after worsening internal political conditions, short-lived Alafins and the Afonja/Ilorin revolt.
- It describes subsequent jihad, warfare and insecurity.
- The chapter says the nineteenth-century wars caused major demographic shifts; Old Oyo, Igboho, Ikoyi, Igbon, Iresa and Oje were destroyed, and people moved from the troubled northern interior southward. New or enlarged centres included Ibadan, Abeokuta, Sagamu, Oke-Odan and Aiyede.

Henry B. Lovejoy's UCLA PhD dissertation (2012), *Old Oyo Influences on the Transformation of Lucumí Identity in Colonial Cuba*, pp.66–67, states that by c.1836 Old Oyo and much of its surrounding district had been deserted/destroyed. It then describes a working alliance of displaced Oyo at Ago Oja (later New Oyo); Atiba obtained support from surviving senior chiefs and warriors and was recognized as Alafin. This is a doctoral synthesis and not a primary enumeration or census.

Peter Morton-Williams, “The Yoruba Kingdom of Oyo,” in Forde & Kaberry (eds.), *West African Kingdoms in the Nineteenth Century* (1967), printed p.45, distinguishes formal political re-establishment: the Oyo Mesi and court officials were in refuge; Atiba detained 34 emissaries at Ago Oja and caused them to install him Alafin there with proper ritual “c.1838”. The text says he then declared Ago Oja to be Oyo, reconstituted offices and replaced absent office-holders.

Samuel Johnson's *The History of the Yorubas* (1921), pp.279–280, presents Atiba's accession after the fall of the ancient capital and the death of Oluewu. The account narrates the crown being offered to Lajuae, his refusal, Atiba's acceptance, and the Oyo Mesi's envoys remaining at Ago through the coronation. It does not give a precise calendar year in that accession passage. The 1921 edition also has an editorial history: Johnson's original manuscript was lost and the published text was reconstructed/edited by Dr O. Johnson from notes and drafts, so it should not be treated as an unmediated primary diary.

The sources can be read as stages:
1. collapse/desertion of Old Oyo and displacement, c.1835–1836;
2. formation of a working refugee/political alliance at Ago Oja;
3. Atiba's formal ritual installation and fuller reconstruction of royal offices, c.1838.

This staged reading reconciles Lovejoy's broad c.1836 “recognized” wording with the more precise c.1838 formal ritual date in Morton-Williams. It also prevents the 1838 reconstructed structure from being projected backwards as a fully consolidated state on the 1836-01-01 simulation anchor. The chronological detail remains circa-year, not a precise day/month finding.
