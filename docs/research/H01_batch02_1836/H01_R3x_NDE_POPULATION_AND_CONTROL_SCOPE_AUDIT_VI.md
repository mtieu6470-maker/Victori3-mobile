## 1. Executive finding

R3x directly re-opened William F. Lye's 1969 article, especially printed pp.88, 91 and 96–97. Lye's estimate of 60,000–80,000 is explicitly a broad estimate of **subjects** of Mzilikazi's polity, not an ethnic Ndebele/Nguni core and not a census. Lye builds it by combining Nguni and Sotho, military and subjugated, tributary and vassal elements. He states that the greater part of Mzilikazi's population consisted of indigenous Sotho and that subject peoples greatly outnumbered the Ndebele. His military figure for 1836 is a separate measure: 1,000–5,000 Nguni warriors, plus subject peoples/subject troops.

A second scholarly trace complicates how confidently the 60,000–80,000 figure can be used. Peter Delius (2010) cites Rasmussen (1978, pp.23–26 and 90–94): Rasmussen's most plausible figure for those who left northern Natal with Mzilikazi in 1821 is 300; by the end of the 1820s, the most reliable estimates put his followers around 20,000, though some sources suggested three or four times that number. Delius explicitly warns that the calculations allow ample room for error. Warren, Hall and Ackermann (2014) likewise repeat a rise from about 300 to around 20,000 by the late 1820s, with significant Sotho-Tswana incorporation, citing Rasmussen.

Thus the evidence contains **different estimates, dates and population universes**:
- about 300 as the initial migrating nucleus (date differs between sources: Delius/Rasmussen says 1821; Lye says by the end of 1823);
- about 20,000 as the most reliable estimate for the followers by the end of the 1820s in Delius's reading of the literature;
- some early estimates three to four times higher (about 60,000–80,000) by the end of that decade;
- Lye's 60,000–80,000 estimate for the wider Mzilikazi polity's subjects, derived from several incorporated categories and evidence around 1829–1836.
These must not be treated as interchangeable, a statistically defined confidence interval, or a dated 1 January 1836 census.

## 2. Lye (1969): direct page-level evidence

William F. Lye, “The Ndebele Kingdom South of the Limpopo River,” *Journal of African History* 10(1) (1969), pp.87–104:

### p.88 — initial following
Lye says that by the end of 1823 Mzilikazi fled Zululand to the interior high veld with about 300 young warriors and women. Lye's footnote traces the 300 figure to Bryant, Mziki and Fynn; the note also mentions that Molema gave 15,000 for Mzilikazi's force, illustrating the spread in early figures. This is a founding/migrant nucleus, not the later polity population.

### p.91 — raids versus control
Lye states that, although Mzilikazi raided beyond the Vaal and Limpopo rivers, he never controlled those areas. This is an explicit warning against converting the full radius of raids or influence into direct territorial control.

### p.96 — 1837 departure, not 1836 population
In the context of Mzilikazi's departure north after the 1836–1837 Boer conflicts, Lye says many subject peoples were allowed to remain behind; 10,000–20,000 people followed him beyond the Limpopo, and the majority of those followers were Sotho. This is a migration stream during the later withdrawal, not a count of the entire polity on 1836-01-01. Do not reuse this as the 1836 entity population.

### p.97 — the 60,000–80,000 subject estimate
Lye describes the territory under Mzilikazi's influence as roughly 200 miles east–west and nearly as far north–south, while stressing that the area he actually utilized was much smaller. He says the first white visitors in 1829 estimated total population at 60,000–80,000. By 1836 Andrew Smith and others estimated the Nguni military force at 1,000–5,000 warriors, with additional subject peoples. Lye explains that the gap between the small Nguni warrior estimate and the kingdom's formidable strength was due to the wider composition of its force and society.

Lye then states that the greater part of Mzilikazi's population consisted of indigenous Sotho. Some were domestic/community labourers; others lived in their own villages, perhaps under traditional chiefs, but under Ndebele regiment surveillance; still others retained substantial domestic freedom but paid tribute and supplied intelligence. He combines Nguni and Sotho, military and subjugated, tributary and vassal categories and calls 60,000–80,000 subjects a reasonable estimate. Footnote 55 says all accounts indicate subject peoples greatly outnumbered the Ndebele.

**Interpretation:** this supports a rough broad-polity subject estimate of 60,000–80,000 in the 1830s, but it is not a headcount of ethnic Ndebele, Nguni core, soldiers alone, or all persons living within an expansive geographic rectangle. The exact stock at 1836-01-01 remains unknown.

## 3. Rasmussen (1978) as traced through Delius (2010)

Peter Delius, “Recapturing Captives and Conversations with ‘Cannibals’: In Pursuit of a Neglected Stratum in South African History,” *Journal of Southern African Studies* 36(1) (2010), pp.7–23, explicitly cites R. Kent Rasmussen, *Migrant Kingdom: Mzilikazi's Ndebele in South Africa* (1978), pp.23–26 and 90–94.

Delius reports:
- Rasmussen's most plausible estimate for the people who left northern Natal with Mzilikazi in 1821 is 300;
- by the end of the 1820s, the most reliable estimates put his followers at around 20,000;
- some sources put the population three or four times higher;
- there is ample room for error in these calculations and further analysis of early state-building dynamics is limited.

Delius then discusses multiple mechanisms of growth and incorporation: refugees from defeated communities, captives, especially children and youths, incorporation into regimental structures, and the importance of women/captives in longer-term population growth. He argues that assimilation language can obscure coercion and the experiences of captives.

**Access limit:** the cited Rasmussen pages are now traced to a peer-reviewed secondary discussion, but the primary book pages themselves have not been directly inspected in this session. Delius's exact estimate and caveat are directly inspected; this does not convert Rasmussen pp.23–26/90–94 into direct source verification.

**Date discrepancy:** Delius attributes the 300-person departure to 1821; Lye p.88 places the flight with about 300 young warriors and women by the end of 1823. Keep both locators and do not silently harmonize the event date.

## 4. Independent scholarly cross-check

Kerryn A. Warren, Simon Hall and Rebecca R. Ackermann, “Craniodental continuity and change between Iron Age peoples and their descendants,” *South African Journal of Science* 110(7–8) (2014), repeat the estimate that a group of about 300 who left KwaZulu-Natal under Mzilikazi increased to about 20,000 by the late 1820s, in the early Ndebele state around present-day Rustenburg. They stress that significant Sotho-Tswana speakers were incorporated as the settlement focus moved west. Their reference 47 is Rasmussen (1978).

This paper is a useful scholarly cross-check for the 300-to-20,000 trajectory and the incorporated Sotho-Tswana component; it is not an independent new census and does not validate a 45,000–85,000 uncertainty interval.
