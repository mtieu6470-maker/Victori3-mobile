# H01 R3aa — NDE demographic source-chain audit: Chase, Archbell and Rasmussen

## 1. Executive result

R3aa improves the source chain behind the competing estimates of Mzilikazi's population around 1829. Three distinguishable lines are now visible:

1. **Chase (1830, printed p.407)** publishes a summary of the 1829 journey of traders Scoon and Luckie and a missionary communication. The report says Malacatzie had about **80,000 people** under him and had conquered land from Batow and other Bichuana tribes, making them dependants. This is a contemporary travel-report estimate, not a census or a systematic enumeration.
2. **Archbell (December 1829)** is independently attested as having encountered Mzilikazi at Plaatberg. The published *Missionary Notices* passage identifies “Motsilakatzi” and records conversation about the Port Natal traders, but the inspected quotation does not give a population number. Lye's notes cite both an Archbell letter and Chase; secondary archival lists give inconsistent box/item descriptions. The original letter has not been inspected.
3. **Rasmussen (1978), as quoted by Kruger (2010, printed p.140)** is said to regard about **20,000 people under Mzilikazi in the Rustenburg area in 1829** as more realistic than the 60,000–80,000 attributed to Methodist missionary Archbell. Rasmussen's original pages were not available, so this remains an indirect quotation through Kruger.

These estimates may not describe the same geography or denominator. Chase's phrase “the country he now possesses” includes conquered Bichuana/Batow dependants; Kruger's reported Rasmussen estimate is explicitly “in the Rustenburg area”. It is unsafe to treat 20,000 and 80,000 as direct competing counts of an identical population universe until the original Rasmussen and Archbell records are read.

## 2. Chase (1830): the 80,000 report

John Centlivres Chase, “Substance of the journal of two trading travellers, and of the communications of a missionary, regarding their recent visits to the countries in the rear of the Portuguese Settlement ot De la Goa Bay,” *South African Quarterly Journal* 1(4) (1830), pp.402–407.

The journal article's metadata identifies John Centlivres Chase as author and explains that it publishes extracts from the journal of the traders Scoon and Luckie, who travelled in 1829 from Campbell's Dorp through Bichuana territory toward the interior behind Delagoa Bay. On printed p.407 the account says Malacatzie had about 80,000 people under him, described as “Zoolah” in language, dress and arms, and that he had conquered the land from Batow and other Bichuana tribes and made them dependants.

**Source assessment:** this is valuable near-contemporary evidence for how an outside visitor described Mzilikazi's power and its incorporated/dependent population. It is not a count produced by a census, roll, village-by-village enumeration or transparent demographic calculation. The 80,000 should be stored as a reported contemporary estimate with its own geographic wording, not as a verified total.

## 3. Archbell (1829): the missionary line and archival locator problem

The accessible 2025 UCT thesis by Angela Ferreira states that in a letter to the Missionary Society dated December 1829, Reverend James Archbell described encountering a “Zula Chief” named “Motsilakatzi” (Mzilikazi) on his journey from Plaatberg. Ferreira also cites the published notice “Missions in Southern Africa,” *Missionary Notices*, vol. VI, no.179 (1829), p.356. The inspected passage concerns the encounter and Port Natal traders; it does not state a population estimate.

Lye (1969) links Archbell's Plaatberg correspondence to the 1829 population discussion:
- footnote 50 cites Chase p.407 and an Archbell Plaatberg item dated 3 December 1829 (date punctuation/reading requires original verification);
- footnote 88 cites an Archbell Plaatberg letter dated 31 December 1829 and gives “WMMS Archives, South Africa, box II, file 1829, no.37”.

A separate published archival bibliography identifies “Methodist Missionary Society Archives (London), South Africa Boxes, S.A., III (1829), no.37, J. Archbell, 31 Dec 1829.” Other OCR of Lye's citations contains unclear box/item typography. These locators may represent separate catalogue systems or transcription/OCR differences; R3aa does not silently harmonize “box II” and “S.A. III”, nor “3 Dec” and “31 Dec”.

**Decision:** the Archbell encounter is supported by a published 1829 notice and a modern scholarly transcription of his December letter. The original manuscript's exact repository, box, file, item number and full demographic wording remain unverified. Do not attribute the Chase 80,000 number to Archbell without reading the letter; do not state that the accessible Missionary Notices excerpt itself gives a population count.

## 4. Rasmussen (1978) through Kruger (2010): the lower estimate

Frans Jacobus Kruger, *Investigation of class 3 and class 4 (Doornspruit) homesteads in the North West Province, South Africa* (M.Phil. thesis, University of Cape Town, 2010), printed p.140, attributes to Rasmussen (1978) the view that by 1829 the number of people under Mzilikazi in the **Rustenburg area** was realistically about 20,000 rather than the 60,000–80,000 estimated by Methodist missionary Archbell. Kruger further discusses incorporation of Sotho/Tswana and Nguni women.

This is a useful scholarly trace to Rasmussen but is not direct verification of Rasmussen's wording or cited pages. The original monograph's pp.23–26 and 90–94 remain unavailable in the accessible Google Books preview. Kruger's narrower geographic phrase “Rustenburg area” is a material distinction from Chase's broad “country he now possesses” and conquered Bichuana dependants. The two figures may reflect different territorial/population coverage, not simply one correct and one incorrect count.

## 5. How this connects to Lye (1969)

William F. Lye, “The Ndebele Kingdom South of the Limpopo River,” *Journal of African History* 10(1) (1969), pp.87–104:
- p.97 reports that first white visitors in 1829 estimated 60,000–80,000; Lye then develops his own broad estimate of 60,000–80,000 **subjects** by combining Nguni and Sotho, military/subjugated, tributary and vassal categories. The greater part of the population was indigenous Sotho.
- p.97 separately gives 1,000–5,000 as estimates of the **Nguni military force** in 1836, in addition to subject peoples. Footnote 51 records differing force estimates: A. Smith 1,000–2,000 trained Ndebele warriors; W. C. Harris 5,000; American missionaries 3,000 for the total Ndebele force. These are military-force observations, not polity population counts.
- footnote 50 expressly connects Chase p.407 and Archbell's Plaatberg correspondence to the 1829 discussion.
- p.91 warns that raids beyond the Vaal and Limpopo did not establish control of those regions.

Lye's 60,000–80,000 composite estimate is not an independent census that validates Chase or Archbell: it incorporates earlier visitor evidence and adds an interpretive synthesis of population categories. Nor should its broad subject total be mistaken for an ethnic Ndebele core or the exact resident population on 1 January 1836.

The dates and units above do not define a reliable demographic growth curve. The initial 300 figure itself has a chronology discrepancy (1821 in Delius/Rasmussen versus by end 1823 in Lye). The “20,000” figure is reported once for Rustenburg-area subjects in Kruger and once for late-1820s followers in Delius; their exact equivalence is not established.
